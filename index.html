How the algorithm works

A genetic algorithm keeps a population of candidate boards and improves them over many generations. It copies the better ones with small random changes, and the worse ones die out.

  1. Candidates. Each candidate is a complete 81-cell board. The puzzle's given digits stay in their cells, and the remaining digits are shuffled over the empty cells. Every candidate therefore holds exactly nine of each digit 1–9, just like the real solution. Only the arrangement can be wrong.
  2. Fitness. Each row, column and 3×3 box adds one point for every digit it is missing, which is the same as one point per repeated digit. A fitness of 0 means no rule is broken: the puzzle is solved. Lower is better.
  3. Selection. Each generation keeps the best of the population unchanged. The rest are bred from parents chosen by tournament: pick candidates at random and keep the fittest.
  4. Mutation. A child starts as a copy of its parent. About half of the children then get the digits in two of the puzzle's empty cells swapped, so the givens never move and the digit counts never change. One of the two cells is always one whose digit clashes with another cell in its row, column or box, which points the search at the mistakes. Some children get several swaps in a row.
  5. Restarts. Candidates can get stuck close to a solution, typically at fitness 2, where no single swap helps. If the best fitness hasn't improved for generations, the population is replaced with fresh random boards.
